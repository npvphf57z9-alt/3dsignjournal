Section 01

How It Started

Where the idea came from

This started like a lot of projects do, with a YouTube tutorial. I found a video about making 3D printed LED signs in Autodesk Fusion 360. It showed how to import SVGs, do offsets, extrude bodies, build diffusers, and make hollow housings for LED strips. It was exactly the kind of thing I wanted to try.

But I didnt want to just copy the tutorial. I had been following WNTD Apparel for a while. I love their streetwear look and that ghost logo. Clean, bold, a little edgy. It felt right for my first big Fusion 360 build.

So I decided to make a custom sign inspired by their style. Typography, branding, and a 3D printed frame built from scratch. I planned to add LED lighting inside but never got the materials for it.

Section 02

Inspiration & Research

What I was going for

WNTD Apparel always stood out to me. Minimalist but bold. Their ghost mascot is simple but iconic. I wanted to capture that energy in something physical, a sign I could actually hang up.

Picking the font was way harder than I expected. A bunch of typefaces looked great on screen but came out too thin when extruded. Others were unreadable at small thicknesses. Some just didnt have the streetwear feel I wanted.

I tested probably a dozen before landing on a bold wide sans-serif. It held its shape during extrusion and matched the WNTD vibe. That was my first real lesson. Aesthetics and manufacturability have to work together from the start.

Section 03

Designing in Fusion 360

From sketches to something I could print

Once I settled on a font, the real work started. I opened Fusion 360, made a new sketch, and imported the SVG files. Laid out the text, scaled it, positioned it for extrusion. Making the outer walls for the LED housing meant careful offset planning. Each letter needed enough space inside for the LED strip while still being strong enough to hold together.

I also designed a diffuser, a thin translucent layer that would go in front of the LEDs to soften the light. That meant nested offsets and separating bodies by function. Every step needed to be precise even though I never got to actually use it.

Once the font was right everything fell into place. The offsets gave clean walls, the hollow interiors fit the strips perfectly, and the geometry was actually printable. Heres the full timeline of how it went down.

Section 04

Problems & What I Did About Them

When things went wrong

Critical Design Oversight

Problem 1: Forgotten Entry/Exit Holes

So the first print of the base finishes and I look at it and my stomach drops. I completely forgot to put entry and exit holes for the LED strip. The whole thing was just a sealed shell. No way to get the LEDs inside without cutting into it.

The print was already done so I had to improvise. Grabbed a heat gun and melted holes between the letter cavities by hand to create channels for the strip. It was messy and imprecise and made assembly way harder than it needed to be.

Lesson learned the hard way: always plan your wiring paths before you print. Forget one thing in CAD and youll be paying for it later.

Size Constraint

Problem 2: The Base and Caps Didn't Fit the Printer

After finishing the full scale design I realized something. The base and caps were too big for my printer. The whole housing was one piece and there was no way it was fitting on the build plate.

Had to go back into Fusion 360 and cut everything into smaller sections. Each piece needed alignment tabs and registration marks so theyd line up when glued together. More parts, more setup, more post processing. Not ideal but it had to be done.

Printed the sections, joined them with adhesive, and lined them up carefully. The photo below shows the pieces before assembly. Not the clean one piece print I wanted, but it was the only way with my printer size.

Section 05

Printing Everything

Making it real

For printing I used standard PLA with a 0.4mm nozzle. Layer height at 0.2mm for a good balance between detail and speed. Infill was set to 15% for most parts, grid pattern. Walls were 2 layers thick, and I used 3 top and bottom layers to keep things solid. Nozzle temp was 210°C, bed at 60°C. Nothing fancy, just reliable default settings that I know work.

The base was printed in a darker filament to create contrast with the front layers. Support settings were the tricky part. Too much support and the inside surfaces came out rough. Too little and the print would fail. Ran a few test prints to find the right balance, ended up using standard supports with a 0.1mm interface gap so theyd peel off cleanly.

Orientation mattered a lot. Some parts printed best face down for a smooth front surface. Others had to be rotated to avoid crazy overhangs. Every piece needed its own approach. The prints themselves came out clean, no major failures after the test runs.

Section 06

Putting It Together

The fun part

Assembly started with cleanup. Sanding rough edges, clearing out support leftovers, making sure all the cavities were clean. The ghost logo sat inside the frame nicely. The internal support bridges made it stiffer, which actually helped. It snapped into place and held tight.

The original plan was to thread an LED strip behind each letter through channels in the base so the light would glow through the front. I had even planned out where the strip would run and designed the hollow spaces for it. But I ended up not being able to get the materials for the lighting, so that part never happened. The housing and the structure are done, just no lights inside.

The wiring, power supply, and final mounting are all on hold for now. The printed parts are assembled and the structure is solid, but the LED part of the project is incomplete. Still glad I got this far though.

Section 07

What I Learned

The stuff that stuck

Looking back I learned way more from this than any tutorial could teach. Planning ahead isnt just about having a plan, its about thinking about what could go wrong before it does. Forgetting to add entry holes for the LED strip I had planned was such a simple mistake but it caused so many problems later.

Now I think about printability from the very first sketch. Supports, surface finish, orientation. These arent afterthoughts. They shape every decision from the start. The ghost logo issue taught me that engineering and design arent opposites. The best solutions serve both.

Patience was probably the biggest lesson. Iteration isnt failure, its just refining. Every test print, every failed offset, every burned hole I made with that heat gun, they all taught me something about how CAD and manufacturing actually work. The mistakes were what I actually learned from.

"Every failed print improved the final design."
Me, after way too many hours

Final Thoughts

Wrapping Up

This project was both creative and technical. It pushed me in ways I didnt expect. Hard at every stage, from picking a font to planning extrusions to cleaning up prints. But it was worth it.

I picked up real CAD skills, got a feel for manufacturing limits, and learned to actually work through problems instead of restarting. Engineering isnt just about getting the numbers right. Every decision affects the final result.

I came out of this with CAD skills, a better understanding of manufacturing, and way more confidence with physical prototyping.

Most importantly I proved to myself that something I designed on a screen can become a real thing that actually works and looks good.

"Took an idea from a screen and turned it into something real that lights up. That feels pretty good."
WNTD LED SIGN

Made with Fusion 360, PLA, and way too much patience.
