Section 01

Project Introduction

The vision behind the build

This project began the way many great engineering challenges do — with a YouTube tutorial. I came across a video explaining how to create 3D printed LED signs using Autodesk Fusion 360. The workflow involved importing SVG files, creating offset geometry, extruding bodies, designing diffusers, and building hollow housings for LED strips. It was the perfect intersection of digital design and physical manufacturing.

But instead of following the tutorial exactly, I decided to make the project my own. I had been following WNTD Apparel for their clean streetwear aesthetic and distinctive ghost logo. The brand's visual identity — minimal, bold, slightly edgy — felt like the perfect inspiration for my first major Fusion 360 project.

The result would be a custom LED sign that combined typography, branding, and functional lighting — wrapped inside a fully 3D printed housing designed from scratch.

Project Goals

  • ◆ Learn Fusion 360 from scratch
  • ◆ Understand extrusion workflows
  • ◆ Design printable geometry
  • ◆ Create LED-compatible hollow structures
  • ◆ Combine engineering + artistic branding

Section 02

Inspiration & Research

Finding the creative direction

WNTD Apparel has always stood out to me for its minimalist yet bold approach to streetwear. The ghost mascot — a simple but iconic silhouette — felt like the perfect subject for a backlit LED sign. The brand's aesthetic is clean, confident, and unmistakable. I wanted to capture that same energy in a physical, illuminated object.

Choosing the font turned out to be one of the most unexpectedly difficult parts of this project. Many typefaces that looked great on screen appeared too thin when extruded. Others lost all readability at small thicknesses. Some simply didn't match the streetwear vibe I was going for.

After testing nearly a dozen options, I selected a bold, wide sans-serif that held its structure during extrusion and matched the WNTD aesthetic perfectly. It was a lesson in itself: in engineering design, aesthetics and manufacturability must coexist from the very beginning.

Section 03

Fusion 360 Design Process

From sketches to printable geometry

Once the font was finalized, the real work began. I started by creating a new sketch in Fusion 360 and importing the SVG reference files. The text was laid out, scaled to the correct dimensions, and positioned for extrusion. Creating the outer walls for the LED housing required careful offset planning — each letter needed enough interior space for the LED strip to pass through while maintaining structural integrity.

The diffuser geometry was a separate challenge. I needed to create a thin, translucent layer that would sit in front of the LEDs to soften the light output. This meant working with nested offsets, separating bodies by function, and constantly testing for printability. The step-by-step process demanded precision at every stage.

After the correct font was selected, everything clicked. The offset tools created clean wall thickness, the hollow interiors fit the LED strips perfectly, and the printable geometry held together. Below is the full workflow timeline documenting every phase of the design.

STEP 01

Research

Studied Fusion 360 tutorials, LED sign workflows, and offset extrusion techniques to understand the full design pipeline.

STEP 02

Font Testing

Tested a dozen typefaces for readability at extrusion depth, structural integrity, and aesthetic fit with the WNTD brand.

STEP 03

SVG Import

Imported vector references into Fusion 360. Aligned and scaled geometry to match the target sign dimensions.

STEP 04

Extrusions

Created extruded bodies from 2D sketches. Established base thickness and wall depth for LED housing structure.

STEP 05

Offset Design

Applied offset tools to create wall thickness. Prepared hollow interiors for LED strip routing and fitment.

STEP 06

Test Printing

Ran small-scale test prints to verify clearances, support requirements, and overall printability of each component.

STEP 07

Final Printing

Launched full-scale prints of the base, ghost logo, text components, and diffuser panels.

STEP 08

Assembly

Post-processing, LED installation, wiring, and final assembly of all printed components. (In progress)

Section 04

Major Problems & Engineering Solutions

When the design fights back

Critical Design Oversight

Problem 1: Forgotten Entry/Exit Holes

During the first print of the base, I realized with a sinking feeling that I had completely forgotten to model entry and exit holes for the LED strip. The entire base had printed as a sealed shell — there was literally no way to get the LED strip inside without modification.

With the print already complete, I had to improvise. I grabbed a heat gun and carefully melted holes between the letter cavities, creating manual channels for the LED strip to pass through. It was messy, inexact, and made the final assembly significantly more complicated.

This mistake taught me an unforgettable lesson: always account for wiring paths before hitting print. A small omission in CAD can become a major headache in the physical world.

Design Iteration

Problem 2: The Ghost Shape Lost Detail

The ghost logo — the centerpiece of the design — lost its recognizable silhouette when the extrusion was made flat. The simple outline that worked so well as a 2D graphic became an unrecognizable blob when given depth.

My first attempt was to offset the geometry downward, but that would have required excessive support material. Worse, the supports would have contacted the visible surface, leaving scars that would ruin the aesthetic finish.

The solution came from rethinking the approach entirely. Instead of downward offsetting, I offset the ghost shape inward and added internal rectangular support bridges. These preserved the ghost silhouette while keeping everything printable — no surface damage, no excess material waste.

❌ Failed Approach

Downward offsetting required excessive supports that damaged the surface finish and ruined the aesthetic quality of the ghost silhouette.

✅ Final Solution

Inward offsetting with internal support bridges preserved the ghost outline while maintaining full printability and surface quality.

Section 05

3D Printing Process

From digital to physical

The printing phase began with slicer setup. I loaded the Fusion 360 exports into the slicer, configured layer heights, infill densities, and support settings for each component. The base was printed separately from the text and ghost elements, using a darker filament to create visual contrast between the housing and the illuminated front layers.

Support settings required careful consideration. Too much support and the interior surfaces would be rough. Too little and the geometry would fail mid-print. I ran several test prints to dial in the optimal balance, adjusting support density and interface clearances with each iteration.

Orientation decisions also played a critical role. Some components printed better face-down for surface quality; others needed to be rotated to minimize overhangs. Every part demanded its own strategy. The assembly is still in progress as of this writing, but the printed components are promising.

Section 06

Assembly

Bringing it all together

Assembly is the phase where digital planning meets physical reality. The printed base needed post-processing — sanding rough edges, clearing support remnants, and ensuring all cavities were free of debris. The LED strip was carefully threaded through the manually created channels (a direct consequence of Problem 1), then positioned behind each letter for even illumination.

The ghost logo component required delicate handling during assembly. The internal support bridges that preserved its silhouette also made it slightly more rigid, which was actually beneficial — it snapped into place with satisfying precision inside the housing frame.

The wiring is still being finalized. Power delivery, connector routing, and mounting hardware are all being tested. This project is very much a work in progress — but each step forward brings the illuminated vision closer to completion.

Section 07

Reflection & Lessons Learned

What the process taught me

Looking back, this project taught me more than any tutorial ever could. I learned that planning ahead isn't just about having a roadmap — it's about anticipating failure points before they happen. Forgetting the LED entry holes was a simple mistake, but it drove home how a single overlooked detail in CAD can cascade into real-world complications.

I now think about printability from the very first sketch. Support structures, surface finish, orientation — these aren't afterthoughts. They're fundamental design constraints that shape every decision. The ghost logo problem taught me that engineering and visual design aren't opposing forces. The best solutions serve both.

Patience was perhaps the most important lesson. Iteration is not failure — it's refinement. Each test print, each failed offset, each overheated hole I melted with a heat gun — they all added to my understanding of CAD workflows and manufacturing limitations. The mistakes were the education.

"Every failed print improved the final design."
— Reflection from the workshop

Final Reflection

Conclusion

This project was both artistic and technical — a rare combination that pushed me to grow in unexpected ways. It was challenging at every stage, from font selection to extrusion planning to manual post-processing. But it was also deeply rewarding.

Through this build, I developed practical CAD skills, a real awareness of manufacturing constraints, and the confidence to tackle iterative problem-solving in physical prototyping. I learned that engineering isn't just about getting the dimensions right — it's about understanding how every decision affects the final result.

The project helped me develop CAD skills, manufacturing awareness, iterative problem-solving, and confidence with physical prototyping.

Most importantly, I proved to myself that a concept born in digital space can be transformed into something real, functional, and beautiful.
